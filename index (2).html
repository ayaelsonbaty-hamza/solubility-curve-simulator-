Key ideas

Solubility is the maximum mass of a solute that dissolves in 100 g of water at a stated temperature. Its units here are g per 100 g water.

A saturated solution holds as much solute as it can at that temperature. Undissolved solid and dissolved ions are in dynamic equilibrium:

MX(s) ⇌ M⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq)

When a solution cools, its solubility falls. The first crystals appear at the temperature where the solution becomes saturated, so at that temperature:

solubility = (mass of salt ÷ mass of water) × 100

Each run gives one point on the curve. Adding more water lowers the concentration, so the next run crystallises at a lower temperature.

Why the curves have different slopes

All three salts dissolve endothermically. By Le Chatelier's principle, raising the temperature moves the equilibrium to the right, so solubility rises. The more endothermic the dissolving, the steeper the curve.

SaltΔsolH / kJ mol⁻¹Solubility at 20 °Cat 80 °C
NaCl+3.8835.937.9
KCl+17.2234.251.3
KNO3+34.8931.6169

ΔsolH depends on the balance between breaking up the lattice (endothermic) and hydrating the ions (exothermic). K⁺ (ionic radius 138 pm) is larger than Na⁺ (102 pm). Its charge density is lower, so it is hydrated less exothermically, and dissolving KCl is more endothermic overall.

Model assumptions

  • Density of water is 1.00 g cm⁻³, so 1.00 cm³ of water has a mass of 1.00 g.
  • Salt dissolves or crystallises almost instantly once the solution is below or above saturation, because the tube is stirred all the time.
  • The water bath reaches at most 98 °C. Cooling in air approaches 22 °C and the ice bath approaches 1 °C.
  • With realistic errors on: the balance reading varies slightly from the target mass, crystals appear 0 to 1.5 °C below the true saturation temperature (supercooling), a little water evaporates above 85 °C, and the thermometer reads to 0.5 °C.
  • Solubility between data-book points is found by smooth interpolation.

Questions

Q1Define the term solubility.Show answer

The maximum mass of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent (water) at a stated temperature, forming a saturated solution. Units: g per 100 g water.

Q2What is a saturated solution? Use the idea of equilibrium.Show answer

A solution that contains the maximum amount of dissolved solute at that temperature. If excess solid is present, the rate of dissolving equals the rate of crystallising: MX(s) ⇌ M⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) is in dynamic equilibrium.

Q3Explain why the temperature at which crystals first appear lets you calculate the solubility.Show answer

Above that temperature the solubility is greater than the concentration in the tube, so all the salt stays dissolved. As the solution cools, solubility falls until it equals the concentration in the tube. The solution is then exactly saturated, and any further cooling makes the excess crystallise. So at the crystallisation temperature, solubility = mass of salt ÷ mass of water × 100.

Q44.00 g of KCl is dissolved in 9.00 cm³ of water. Crystals first appear at 55.5 °C. Calculate the solubility of KCl at 55.5 °C.Show answer

Mass of water = 9.00 cm³ × 1.00 g cm⁻³ = 9.00 g

Solubility = 4.00 ÷ 9.00 × 100 = 44.4 g per 100 g water

Q5Using data-book values, a saturated solution of KCl in 50 g of water is cooled from 80 °C to 20 °C. Calculate the mass of crystals that forms.Show answer

At 80 °C: 51.3 g per 100 g, so 51.3 × 50 ÷ 100 = 25.65 g dissolved.

At 20 °C: 34.2 g per 100 g, so 34.2 × 50 ÷ 100 = 17.10 g can stay dissolved.

Mass of crystals = 25.65 − 17.10 = 8.55 g (8.6 g)

Q6Compare your NaCl and KCl curves. Which salt's solubility depends more on temperature? Explain why.Show answer

KCl. Its solubility rises from about 28 to 56 g per 100 g between 0 and 100 °C. NaCl only rises from about 35.7 to 39.0 g per 100 g.

Dissolving KCl is more endothermic (+17.22 kJ mol⁻¹) than dissolving NaCl (+3.88 kJ mol⁻¹). Raising the temperature favours the endothermic direction, so the equilibrium for KCl shifts further towards the dissolved ions. K⁺ is larger than Na⁺, with lower charge density, so its hydration enthalpy is less exothermic. This makes the overall enthalpy of solution more endothermic.

Q7Why is it hard to get a good NaCl curve with this method? How could you improve it?Show answer

NaCl's solubility changes by only about 0.03 g per 100 g for each °C. A tiny change in the volume of water changes the crystallisation temperature a lot. With 9 g of NaCl in about 24 cm³ of water, a 0.05 cm³ burette error shifts the temperature by about 2 °C. Random errors in volume and temperature can be as large as the trend.

Improvements: use a larger mass of salt and water, add water in small portions (0.20 to 0.30 cm³), heat and cool each run twice and take a mean temperature, and use a digital thermometer or data logger.

Q8Supercooling and evaporation are two sources of error. Explain how each one affects your calculated solubility values or your curve.Show answer

Supercooling: crystals may not form until the solution is a little below its true saturation temperature. The recorded temperature is too low, so each point is plotted too far left. The curve then sits above the true curve (solubility looks too high at a given temperature). Stirring and cooling slowly reduce this.

Evaporation: water lost while heating means the real mass of water is less than the burette volume. The true concentration is higher than calculated, so the calculated solubility is too low. Keep heating times short and use a loosely stoppered tube.
